STATE OF RHODE ISLAND AND PROVIDENCE PLANTATIONS 
BOARD OF ELECTIONS  
 
DIRECTIVE 
 The State Board of Elections (hereinafter "Board") issues this Directive pursuant to R.I. 
Gen. Laws § 17-7-5(c).  The Directive addresses the circumstance in which a person who has 
been elected to either the House or Senate of the General Assembly refuses to serve in that 
office.  The issue is governed by provisions found in the Constitution of Rhode Island and our 
General Laws.   
 At any time between the day of his or her election to the General Assembly and the 
beginning of the term of said office, a person can submit a written declaration to the Secretary of 
State indicating that s/he refuses to serve in that office.  R.I. Gen. Laws § 17-3-5(a).   Upon 
receipt of the written declaration, the Secretary of State "will cause a warrant to be issued 
ordering a new election" for that seat.  Id.  The calendar for the special election is set by the 
Board, pursuant to R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 17-7-5(a)(7) and 17-15-3 (“In the event of a special 
election, the state board shall fix the date or dates on which the various party primary elections 
shall be held; provided that all the party elections shall be held by the thirtieth (30th) day 
preceding the date fixed for the special election… .”) .  The election must be held at least 70 but 
not more that 90 days from the submission of the declaration.  R.I. Gen. Laws § 17-3-5(a).   
 A related question concerns representation of the senate or representative district after a 
person files the declaration.  Based upon provisions of both the Constitution and General Laws, 
the incumbent senator or representative remains in office until a successor is elected and 
qualified to serve:   
The senators and representatives in the general assembly shall be elected on the 
Tuesday after the first Monday in November, biennially in even numbered years, 
and shall severally hold their offices for two (2) years from the first Tuesday of 
 2 
January next succeeding their election and until their successors are elected and 
qualified. 
R.I. Const. Art. IV, § 1 (emphasis provided);  see also, R.I. Gen. Laws § 22-3-15.  Thus, the 
General Assembly seat does not become vacant so long as the prior incumbent remains, as that 
incumbent is held over until his or her successor is able to take the seat.  This interpretation has 
been confirmed on multiple occasions by the Rhode Island Supreme Court.  See Casey v. Willey, 
151 A.2d 369, 373 (R.I. 1959) (“where the term of an officer is for a period of years and until his 
successor is elected and qualified, no vacancy results in the office by reason of the failure to elect 
a successor at the time provided by law, since the incumbent continues to hold over until his 
successor is duly elected and qualified”); State ex rel. Tanner v. Perry, 27 A. 606, 607 (R.I. 
1893) (finding that the General Assembly may provide in cases where a special election must be 
called, and that “when such provisions are exhausted, then, again, the incumbent holds over 
under the first section of article 8, and there is no vacancy in the office.”); In re North Smithfield 
Election, 27 A. 597, 599 (R.I. 1893) (“It may also be said that the constitution, in article 8, § 1, 
provides, as far as may be, that no town shall be without a representation, by allowing a senator 
or representative to hold his office until another is 'legally chosen and duly qualified' to fill the 
place, and so there is no vacancy.”).   
 The North Smithfield Election case is particularly instructive, as it involved a senator-
elect who passed away prior to taking office, and addressed the issue of whether a vacancy 
existed as of the senator-elect’s death, such that the town clerk could call for a new election 
immediately.  In re North Smithfield Election, 27 A. at 598.  The Court held that, though the 
incumbent senator did hold over in his office, he did so by virtue of his membership in the prior 
Assembly.  Id. at 599.  Though much of the law has changed, the underlying principle and the 
constitutional text have not materially changed, and therefore the rule applies today:  the 
 3 
incumbent senator or representative holds over until such time as a special election can be held 
and a successor qualified and sworn into office.

The State of Rhode Island Board of Elections (hereinafter “Board of Elections”) hereby 
issues this Directive to the local canvassing authorities for each city and town, pursuant to R.I. 
Gen. Laws § 17-7-5(c).   
PURPOSE FOR DIRECTIVE – FEDERAL PRESENCE AT POLLING PLACES 
This Directive provides guidance and instruction for all polling place Moderators, early 
voting officials, and poll workers regarding the presence of individuals claiming or appearing to 
be federal personnel, including federal law enforcement officers, at Rhode Island’s polling places 
during an election.   
While it is neither common nor anticipated, it is possible that federal law enforcement 
officers may have a presence at or near polling places. Federal law enforcement could include 
officials or agents from DOJ, the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), the U.S. Department of 
Homeland Security (DHS), Customs and Border Protection (CBP), or Immigration and Customs 
Enforcement (ICE). Both federal and state law protect the orderly conduct of elections and 
prohibit interference with voting at or near polling places, including by federal law enforcement 
officials. Federal statutes prohibit armed federal officer presence at election sites, 18 U.S.C. § 
592, and voter intimidation, 18 U.S.C. § 594 and 52 U.S.C.§§ 10101(b), 10307(b). Likewise, 
Rhode Island law prohibits intimidation or disruptive conduct at polling places, R.I. Gen. Law § 
17-23-5 and 17-23-17(a)(7), including interference with the right to vote by the presence of 
federal immigration law enforcement within polling places during voting, R.I. Gen. Laws § 17-
19-55.  
 
 
2 
 
The Board of Elections bears statutory responsibility for the fair and uniform 
administration and enforcement of election laws in the State of Rhode Island.  R.I. Gen. Laws § 
17-7-5(a).   
In order to maintain consistent polling place procedures, to safeguard election integrity, 
and to protect the rights of Rhode Island voters, local canvassing authorities are directed to 
adhere to the procedures set forth in this Directive.  Local canvassing authorities should contact 
the Board of Elections should they need further clarification of these procedures. 
The guidance and instruction herein apply to the polling place as well as the protected 
200-foot perimeter of the polling place. R.I. Gen. Laws § 17-19-21(c). 
PROTOCOL FOR REPORTING INCIDENTS INVOLVING FEDERAL PERSONNEL  
Whether federal personnel may be legally present at or near a polling place depends on 
that federal employee or officer’s role and the activities in which they are engaged. For example, 
it is common for congressional election observers and U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) 
monitors to observe federal election activities. However, the presence of federal law enforcement 
officials at or near polling places is not common and may violate federal or state law.   
In the event that a polling place or early voting official learns of the presence of 
individuals claiming or appearing to be federal personnel at the polling place, the polling place 
Moderator, also referred to as the “Warden” in some cities and towns, or early voting official, 
should first attempt to determine that the individuals are actually federal employees or officers, 
and, if so, what type of employee. The Moderator, or early voting official, may – if they deem it 
safe to do so – approach the person who is believed to be federal personnel and seek 
confirmation as to their identity, their agency employer, or other documentation.  
 
 
3 
 
DOJ MONITORS AND CONGRESSIONAL ELECTION OBSERVERS 
Federal election monitors sent by the U.S. Department of Justice should generally follow 
the same rules and procedures as any member of the public, meaning they are generally 
prohibited from, among other things, unauthorized entry to election locations, talking to voters 
within the polling place, interfering with the conduct of voting, or leaving the designated area for 
observers.  
Congressional election observers sent by Congress, on the other hand, are entitled to 
greater access than members of the public. They will have documentation identifying themselves 
as congressional observers, and may access voting, tabulating, canvassing, and certification areas 
and may ask questions. They, however, cannot handle ballots or election equipment, advocate 
for any position or candidate, take action to reduce ballot secrecy or voter privacy, or interfere 
with a voter’s ability to cast their vote or an election official’s ability to carry out their duties.  
If federal monitors sent by DOJ or congressional observers seek access to equipment, 
ballots, advocate for any position or candidate, take action to reduce ballot secrecy or voter 
privacy, or interfere with a voter’s ability to cast their vote or an election official’s ability to carry 
out their duties, poll workers should calmly and politely inform such individuals that the access 
or activity is not permitted and immediately elevate the issue by informing local law 
enforcement, and also immediately notify the local board of canvassers, and the Board of 
Elections.   A written report of the incident must be submitted to the Board of Elections within 72 
hours. 
  
 
4 
 
FEDERAL LAW ENFORCEMENT 
Whether federal law enforcement may be legally present at or near a polling place 
depends on the activities in which they are engaged. If federal law enforcement officers appear at 
or close to a polling place, the Moderator, or early voting official, should first attempt to 
determine that the individuals are actually federal law enforcement officers. If the Moderator, or 
early voting official, deems it safe and feels comfortable doing so,  they may approach the 
officer(s) and seek confirmation as to their identity, their agency employer, or other 
documentation. However, poll workers, polling place Moderators, and early voting officials are 
not responsible for assessing the validity of the federal officers’ activities, nor should they 
interfere with law enforcement. Furthermore, the poll workers, polling place Moderator, or early 
voting official should elevate the issue immediately. Poll workers should inform their polling 
place Moderator, or early voting official, immediately, and the Moderator, or early voting 
official, should first immediately contact local law enforcement and then immediately contact the 
local board of canvassers and the Board of Elections.  
If safe to do so, the Moderator, or early voting official, may attempt to minimize the 
disruption to voters and the election site, including by politely asking federal law enforcement to 
conduct conversations away from voters. In the same way, and if safe to do so, they may also 
request that the federal officers minimize disruption of polling place activities, pause their 
activity, and/or remain outside of the polling place until local law enforcement or counsel is able 
to arrive. 
The polling place Moderator, or early voting official, should document any adverse 
impact observed by the polling place officials that arises from federal law enforcement officers’ 
conduct, including any type of harassment, intimidation, or interference with any person’s effort 
 
5 
 
to cast a ballot at the polling place or deposit a ballot into a drop box receptacle located within 
the polling place. The Moderator, or early voting official, should also approach any voter who 
interacted directly or indirectly with federal law enforcement officers in order to determine if 
they will voluntarily provide information concerning the encounter, including the voter’s 
identity. In making these observations, the polling place Moderator, or early voting official, 
should not interfere with the federal law enforcement officer.  A written report of the incident 
must be submitted to the Board of Elections within 72 hours. 
Prior to the opening of the polling place, or the start of early voting, the local canvassing 
authorities shall disseminate the attached instructions to each polling place Moderator or early 
voting official, for responding to a federal law enforcement situation, which includes the reported 
or substantiated appearance of ICE agents. Each local board of canvassers shall input the 
appropriate contact information into the instructions before dissemination.

Memo 
To: Political Parties, Candidates, Public Observers 
From: Miguel J. Nuñez, Executive Director 
Date: 10/16/2024 
Re: Guidance Regarding Poll Checkers, Runners, & Watchers,  
and Observers 
 
Background: 
The Board of Elections voted unanimously on October 15, 2024, to issue the 
following guidance regarding the presence of poll Checkers, Runners 
Watchers, and Observers pursuant to Rhode Island General Laws  
§ 17-19-22, 17-15-22 and Board Regulation 410-RICR-20-00-12.   
 
This memo was compiled partially based on a Fact Sheet compiled by the 
Board of Canvassers Working Group issued on September 25, 2024. 
 
Official Recognition: 
• Poll Checkers and poll Watchers are only officially recognized if they 
bear proper credentials from the proper ward or town committee party 
chairperson. 
• A letter from the proper ward or town committee party chairperson 
may qualify as accepted credential. 
• Without these credentials, individuals have no legal standing to act as 
poll Checkers or poll Watchers. 
 
 
 
  
 
 
Poll Checkers: 
• Poll Checkers shall be allowed to sit for the purpose of keeping track of 
those who are voting.  These may be changed during the day.  
• A representative, known as a Runner, of each of the parties shall be 
allowed to come to the table at frequent intervals for the purpose of 
taking whatever list or memoranda the Checkers may wish to give the 
runner. 
 
Poll Watchers: 
• The proper ward or town committee party chairperson is allowed to 
appoint one poll Watcher per polling location. 
• Poll Watchers and are the only designees who have the right to 
challenge the identity of a voter, other than Election Officials. 
 
Observers: 
• The public may be present in the polling place to observe the conduct 
of an election. Only voters and election officials are allowed in the 
Railed or Enclosed Voting Area. No person(s) may willfully hinder the 
orderly conduct of an election. 
 
Voter Challenges: 
• Challenges made by poll Watchers must be based on "good cause" and 
will be evaluated by the polling place Moderator/Warden. 
• If the challenge is accepted by the Moderator/Warden, the voter will be 
required to cast a provisional ballot. 
 
• The Moderator/Warden is responsible for notifying the local Board of 
Canvassers whenever a challenge is made. 
 
• Poll Watchers may not make frivolous challenges. Frivolous challenges 
may lead to poll Watchers being removed from the polling place and 
credentials being voided. The poll Watcher is not permitted to disrupt 
the voting process. 
 
 
Procedures and Collaboration: 
• A table and chairs must be provided outside the enclosed space by the 
city/town for the party representatives known as Checkers and 
Watchers. In the absence of party representatives, the public may use 
the table and chairs. 
 
 
• Local Boards of Canvassers should inform all board members, staff, 
local party chairs and police departments of the rules and guidelines 
concerning poll Checkers, Runners, Observers and Watchers to ensure 
proper understanding and coordination. 
 
Important Notes: 
• Only poll Watchers (with credentials) can challenge the identity of a 
voter. 
• All challenges must be reviewed and determined by the 
Moderator/Warden. 
• Clear communication between Boards of Canvassers, election staff, and 
local law enforcement is essential for smooth polling operations. 
 
This memo summarizes the official procedures and guidelines for poll 
Checkers, poll Watchers, poll Runners poll Observers as determined by the 
Rhode Island Board of Elections. For further information, contact the Board of 
Elections at 401-222-2345 or boe.elections@elections.ri.gov. 





Title 17

Chapter 19
Conduct of Election and Voting Equipment, and Supplies
R.I. Gen. Laws § 17-19-22
§ 17-19-22. Party checkers, runners, and watchers.
The officers required to furnish and equip any voting place shall also provide a table in the room where the voting is
conducted, outside the enclosed space near the first bipartisan pair of supervisors, at which a representative of each
recognized political party bearing credentials signed by the proper ward or town committee chairperson, shall be allowed to
sit for the purpose of keeping track of those who are voting, and these representatives, who shall be known as “checkers,”
may be changed during the day. A representative, known as a “runner,” of each of the parties shall be allowed to come to the
table at frequent intervals for the purpose of taking whatever list or memoranda the checkers may wish to give the runner. A
representative of each recognized political party bearing credentials signed by the proper ward or town committee
chairperson, shall also be allowed outside the enclosed place to observe the voting and assist the checkers, and these
representatives shall be known as “watchers.” The watchers and any election official shall have the right to challenge the
right to vote of any person offering himself or herself as a voter.

§ 17-15-22. Party checkers, runners and watchers.
The local board shall, by written rule, provide for the presence of party checkers to be seated outside the rail next to the
warden to make a list of those who vote and for the distribution of lists or memoranda to the proper representatives or
candidates or groups of candidates. Party checkers, runners and watchers shall be regulated in the same manner as directed

It may not be necessary for the voter to write-in both the firs t and last name of the person being voted for.  However, it is 
always necessary for the last name of the person being voted for to b e written in.  Moreover, it shall be necessary for the voter 
to write-in the first and last name of the person being voted f or when it is apparent that there is more than one person in a race 
with the same last name.  The fo llowing example illustrates when it may or may not be necessary to include both the first and 
last name of the person being voted for. 
Situation:  Maria Sanchez is seeking to be elected to the School Committ ee via write-in votes.  It is widely known that she 
has been campaigning and is asking voters to cast a write-in vo te for her for School Committee.  Although there are several 
other persons named Sanchez in the community, there are no othe r persons named Sanchez who are seeking write-in votes for 
School Committee. 
Manner In Which Voter Wrote Name:  The voter casting a write-in vote wrote the name “Sanchez” in the appropriate space 
(and filled-in the oval next to the name.) 
Local Board Assessment:  In attempting to “reasonably ascertain” the person for whom t o count the vote, the local board can 
“reasonably ascertain” that the person for whom the voter was c asting his/her vote was “Maria Sanchez”, the same person 
who was campaigning to be elected to the School Committee via write-in votes. 
Other Considerations:  If, in this example, more t han one Sanchez were seeking votes for School Committee it would not be 
possible to “reasonably ascertain” which Sanchez the voter was casting his/her vote for.  Thus, in this case, it would be 
necessary for the voter to distinguish which Sanchez he/she was voting for.  (i.e. Writing in the first name of the person he/she 
wished to cast a ballot for.) 
 
2.  Is the use of stickers or labels permitted in the execution of a write-in vote? 
  
The use of stickers and labels is prohibited.  Persons wishing to cast a write-in vote must write in the name of the person for 
whom they wish to vote for in the appropriate space using the appropriate marking device. 
 
3.  When casting a write-in vote, must the voter correctly spel l the name of the person he/she is casting a write-in vote 
for? 
 
Solely because a name is misspelled should not be cause for not  counting a vote for a particular person.  Once again, if the 
local board can “reasonably ascertain” who the voter wished to cast his/her vote for the vote should be counted for that person.  
(i.e. There is a person seeking write-in votes by the name of T homas Brady.  In tabulating the write-in votes the local board 
discovers that there are a preponderance of write-in votes for Tom Brady, Tomas Brady, Tom Bradi, Thomas Bradee, etc.  
Unless there is another person in the race whose name is spelle d somewhat close to that of Thomas Brady the local board 
could “reasonably ascertain” that the voter in each of these cases wished to cast his vote for Thomas Brady.) 
 
4.  Is the use of a “nickname” permitted when casting a write-in vote? 
 
A “nickname” alone is not sufficient to “reasonably ascertain” who the voter wishes to cast his/her vote for.  However, a 
nickname used in conjunction with the last name of the person f or whom the voter wishes to vote for is permitted.  (i.e. The 
name of the person for whom a voter wishes to cast a write-in v ote for is Manual Santiago.  Mr. Santiago is known to his 
friends as “Manny” or “the Barber.”  If the voter writes in Man ny Santiago, the vote should be counted.  However, if the voter  
writes in “Manual the Barber” or simply “Manny” or “The Barber”, the vote should not be counted for Manuel Santiago.)
